# Scaling the one-lift result: 9×9 → 16×16 → 25×25 (2026-11-24)

**Question.** Generalized Sudoku (N×N, N = b²) is NP-complete as N grows. One lift is a
polynomial-time computation, so unless P = NP it cannot force the answer on every large puzzle.
Where does it first stop forcing?

**Answer so far: we have not found a puzzle where it fails.** Every puzzle that needed a lift was
forced by one lift: 38 at 9×9 (16 hand-picked hard ones plus 22 random), 5 at 16×16 (including
two near-minimal ones), and 3 at 25×25 (the largest: 1,091 candidates, 92 min). What does grow is the **cost of the lift**, much faster
than ordinary backtracking.

The 9×9 random sample also ran the forcing audit (most belief the lift can put on wrong claims):
at most 3e-5 for the vector lift, versus up to 37.5 for the LP. The LP forced only 8 of the 22.
The 16×16 and 25×25 runs skip the audit and the wave to save time. Their forcing is read from
the lift's own beliefs (every empty cell > 0.99, all matching the solution).

## What the numbers say

1. **Forcing has not broken yet.** The lift's geometry pinned down the unique solution on every
   puzzle that needed it, at all three sizes, including near-minimal 16×16 puzzles.
2. **The cost tracks difficulty more than size.** A 25×25 lift with 927 candidates took 2 min
   (backtracking: 2 guesses). A 16×16 lift with 650 candidates took 42 min (backtracking: 1,506
   guesses). Puzzles that are hard for search are also slow for the solver.
3. **The wall shows up as cost, not failure.** Going from 374 to 667 candidates on the same
   16×16 grid, backtracking time grew about 190× (9 ms → 1.7 s) and the lift time grew about 110×
   (39 s → 71 min). Backtracking stays about 1,400–4,300× faster in absolute terms.
4. **Why the solver slows down (a hypothesis).** When the answer is forced, the lift's feasible
   set shrinks to a single point with no interior, and first-order solvers like SCS are known to
   converge slowly on such degenerate problems. Observed: at looser tolerance (1e-3 or 1e-4)
   with a 5,000-step cap, SCS did not converge on three hard 9×9 puzzles and nothing popped.
   At tolerance 1e-5 it converged (Inkala's puzzle took 2,875 steps) and every cell popped. I
   have not isolated how much of the slowdown comes from this degeneracy.
5. **This proves nothing about P vs NP.** These sizes are small, and puzzles generated by random
   clue removal are typically far from the hardest instances of their size. Hard instances of
   NP-complete problems are believed to exist at every large size. Unless P = NP, a fixed lift
   must eventually stop forcing (or the forcing must become unreachable at the needed precision).

## Next questions this opens

- **Find a failure.** Search for 16×16 or 25×25 puzzles that are hard for search, for example
  by selecting the ones with the most backtracking guesses from many generated puzzles, or by
  encoding known hard SAT families as Sudoku-like constraint problems. Then check whether one
  lift still forces them.
- **A faster lift.** Use a low-rank (Burer–Monteiro) or structure-exploiting solver so 25×25
  near-minimal puzzles (1,100+ candidates) are reachable.
- **Certificates.** Turn "forced up to tolerance" into an exact rational dual certificate per
  puzzle. That ties to the certified-computation work in this repository.

## Reproduce

```bash
P=/Volumes/KINGSTON/Vibecoding/.venvs/sdp/bin/python
$P gw_scale.py 3 40 909 5000 scale_9x9.json                    # 9x9 random sweep (with audit)
$P gw_scale.py ladder 4 7 104 2000 100000 ladder16_s7_t104.json  # one 16x16 rung
$P gw_scale.py ladder 5 11 315 2000 100000 ladder25_s11_t315.json # one 25x25 rung
```

Each `ladder*.json` stores the puzzle, its solution, and every number above. Logs:
`scale_9x9.log`, `ladder16.log` (it also contains three launch errors from a shell
word-splitting mistake; those runs were relaunched), and `ladder25.log`.
