Balance at every scale

Before anything tips, every level flickers with small swings that die away. A system poised at a critical point looks like this: fluctuations at all sizes at once, nested inside each other.

A tip that cannot undo itself

Each fold sits in a shallow dip at the top of its hill. Most swings fall back. One that crosses the rim keeps going, the way one ice crystal in supercooled water turns the whole glass to ice. Physicists call it nucleation.

The cascade carries the pattern

A tipped fold leans on its container and on the folds inside it, so the tip spreads up and down the levels. Folds that tipped independently fell in different directions, and those differences are carried into the unfolded world, much as inflation is thought to have stretched tiny early fluctuations into the seeds of galaxies.

Scars that carry whole numbers

Where regions that fell in different directions meet, the colors can wind all the way around a point. That point is a scar. It winds a whole number of times, which is the same counting that defines the circle in homotopy type theory, and it can only disappear by meeting a scar that winds the opposite way. At its core the field is zero: a speck of the original balance survives there.

Ripples by size, and an honest near-miss

Cosmologists split the microwave sky's ripples by size and ask how much variation each size carries. The answer is almost an even split: a tilt of n ≈ 0.965, with slightly more in the large ripples. The panel makes the same split. Before anything tips, the noise sits in the smallest ripples and n is near 3. The cascade moves the variation into large ripples, so at the unfolding n is well below 1. As the scars heal, a few broad swirls are left behind and n dips to around zero or below. Then the world settles near 1. In five test runs with 5 levels, the long-run average read 0.96 to 0.99 after 8,000 time units, still creeping up as the early settling faded.

That closeness is a rhyme, not a reproduction. Turning the direction a region fell costs nothing. In a flat two-dimensional world, the noise of such a free direction spreads evenly across sizes, the same reason inflation predicts near-evenness. The details here come from the grid. The largest ripples come in only a few coarse shapes, so the directions alone read about 0.91. Jitter in how far each cell has fallen sits in the smallest ripples and lifts the total to about 1. If a scar never finds its opposite, as happened in test runs with 6 levels, its swirl dominates every size and n stays near −1.2. Defects leave a fingerprint the real sky does not show, which is part of why cosmic strings were ruled out as the main seed of galaxies. The sky's 0.965 comes from the slowly changing field of inflation, and this model has nothing like it.

A toy model, not a theory of the universe. Each fold follows a potential with a metastable balanced state and a deeper tipped ring; tipped folds push their neighbors in the nesting tree. After unfolding, the innermost folds become a grid whose neighbors pull on each other. The run is fully determined by its seed and settings.
