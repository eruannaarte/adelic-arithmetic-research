Space from a Tangle

Start with 216 points, every one linked to every other: no distance, no shape, no dimension. Cool them under local rules that ask each point for a few links and each link to close a few small squares. Then send a random walker through what is left and ask how many dimensions it feels.

Rules

Each rule is satisfied exactly by a perfect grid of that dimension. Triangles always cost extra. A move that makes or breaks no sticky contact costs nothing, so it still happens at the lowest temperature. Like the loops of a real enzyme, those parts stay mobile while the core is frozen.
Heat lets the network accept changes that break the rules. A 3D crystal started at 1.5 and cooled survives; held at 1.5 it slowly melts, and at 1.75 it melts within seconds.

What the walker feels

    A walker that wanders s steps returns to its start less often in more dimensions. The slope of that decline is the dimension it feels. Reference grids have the same 216 points.

    Moments

      What the full-size runs found

      The same model in C, with 512 points, starting from all 130,816 links. Every number below comes from those runs.

      The squares rule is what makes space

      With only a rule about how many links each point has, the network reaches its lowest energy as a tangle. Its dimension keeps climbing the farther the walker goes, the signature of a network where everything is a few steps from everything else.

      Asking each link to close squares changes that. The 1D rule reaches its perfect state exactly: rings, dimension 1. The 3D rule freezes into a glass that reads about 2.4 at every scale. Cooled fifteen times more slowly, it forms dense grains strung on narrow necks, which look 2.4-dimensional up close and 1-dimensional from far away.

      The 2D rule budded off bubble universes

      The 2D rule asks for a flat sheet: 4 links per point, each on 2 squares. It never found one. Instead the network broke into 45 closed pieces, and 22 of them are perfect cubes.

      A cube is a sphere tiled by squares. Euler's formula says any sphere tiled by squares, where points have 3 or 4 links, has exactly 8 corners with 3 links: the sum of (4 − links) over all points is 8. Each bubble is flat almost everywhere and keeps all of its curvature in those 8 corners.

      A time direction keeps space whole, but thin

      Causal dynamical triangulations found a 4-dimensional universe only after building spacetime with a time direction, so the model got one: each point lives at one of 8 moments, and links may only join a moment to itself or to its neighbors.

      Time alone moved the bubbles inside the moments. Forbidding space to tear kept each moment in one piece, but a stringy one, barely more than a tree. Strong worldlines pushed the spacetime reading toward the crystal's without making any moment a sheet.

      Space survives, but is not born

      The perfect crystal is the rules' lowest-energy state. Warmed to 1.5 and cooled again, it survives almost untouched. Held at 1.75 it melts, and the melt never refreezes into a crystal; it supercools into the same glass the complete graph makes.

      So the last test planted a seed: half the world as perfect crystal, half liquid. No seed ever grew. Above about 0.8 the seed melts from its surface. Below that, the liquid stops rearranging. With a time direction the seed survives better, but the liquid moments never become sheets.

      The tiles say why. Disordered wirings outnumber the crystal by a factor of about e4,700. The crystal's energy advantage only wins once it is cold, and by then nothing moves.

      A toy model, not a theory of the universe. Energy = Σ(links − target)² + Σ over links (squares − target)² + 2 × triangles. Moves are local: remove a link, add a link two steps away, or slide a link's end one step; 1% toggle a random pair. The live network uses 216 points so it runs in a browser; the findings use 512. The folding chain is the HP model of protein folding (Dill, 1985) on a cubic grid: sticky beads that touch lower the energy by 0.5 each, and finding a chain's best fold in this model is NP-complete. Ideas behind it: quantum graphity and geometrogenesis (Konopka, Markopoulou, Smolin), causal dynamical triangulations (Ambjørn, Jurkiewicz, Loll). Companion to The First Tipping.
